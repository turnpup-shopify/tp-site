A design practice for rich, intentional lives full of feeling. 
 
 Studies show that the way a space[3](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-3) is designed impacts mental health, physical well-being, creativity, and self-reflection—as a _[New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/mg25433823-000-how-interior-design-choices-can-boost-your-mental-and-physical-health/)_ report summarizes:  

- Hospital patients whose rooms overlook natural settings tend to need lower doses of painkillers and leave hospitals earlier than those whose windows look out over a brick wall.[4](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-4)
    
- Students who can gaze out over green views while studying exhibit higher levels of mental focus.[5](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-5)
    
- People surrounded by wood-based furniture feel happier and less fatigued,[6](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-6) and being able to feel or smell wood decreases blood pressure[7](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-7) and relaxes the body.[8](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-8)
    
- The layout of a building or room influences the level of relaxation or anxiety one feels: open-plan designs lead to more relaxation, while smaller, enclosed spaces produce higher anxiety levels.[9](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-9)
    
- Lighting is important too: dimmer lighting leads to less self-consciousness and higher levels of creativity,[10](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-10) while bright lighting encourages self-reflection.[11](https://www.verypublicsecretsociety.com/p/good-design-might-save-the-world#footnote-11)


**The central tension: defamiliarization vs. the ordinary as ordinary.** One camp (Leddy, Dewey-inspired) says everyday aesthetics requires making the familiar strange to reveal hidden richness. Critics say this loses the very everyday-ness; the harder task is appreciating comfort, hominess, and good functionality _as_ familiar — or acknowledging monotony as a real (negative) aesthetic quality.
